# Designing one 45-minute experience for a four-year-old and a nine-year-old

Siblings play together. The same session has to hold a child who cannot read and
a child who will notice a plot hole. Almost every design rule below exists to
resolve that tension rather than to split the difference.

---

## Two levels of story, both correct

| A four-year-old follows | A nine-year-old also follows |
|---|---|
| A magic portal took a dragon egg. We found it. We saved the dragon. | Someone opened that portal deliberately. Who? |

The surface plot resolves completely. The larger question is glimpsed twice and
never explained: a robed silhouette on the far side of the portal, and a symbol
that recurs at the end. **Neither reading is the "real" one**, and the younger
child loses nothing by not noticing.

The discipline this needs is restraint. The temptation at every beat is to have
the guide explain the mystery, which would convert a nine-year-old's inference
into a four-year-old's exposition and ruin it for both.

---

## A newly learned spell always works

The first version had the fire spell fail comically before it succeeded, because
failure is funny and builds tension.

It is also the wrong lesson. **A child who has just learned fire and watches it
do nothing has learned that their new power is unreliable.**

So the rule became: the spell succeeds exactly as intended, and **its success
creates the next problem.** Fire breaks the blocked way open *and sets it
alight*, which traps a creature behind the flames, which is what makes the second
spell necessary. The plot is driven by consequence rather than by failure, and
the child is never the reason the story stalls.

The laugh moved rather than disappeared. It now lives in the guide's reaction to
the fire she encouraged: *"...that worked. That REALLY worked. Okay. New
problem."*

---

## Nothing gates on recognising a word

Three moments listen for speech: two spell words and a riddle answer. **None of
them blocks progress on recognising a specific word**, because a microphone that
does not work must not be able to end an adventure at minute 37.

The riddle is the clearest case:

- **Silence costs no attempt.** Twenty quiet seconds are a child thinking, not
  two wrong answers, and telling them "almost" about silence is a lie.
- **No countdown.** A gesture is a performance and needs a timer. Thinking is not.
- **Hearing and correctness are different signals.** A ring responds to sound
  arriving, always, so a child knows they were heard even when they were wrong.
- **After two attempts the character says the answer with them** and the story
  continues either way.

The answer is one word drawn from a fixed five-word vocabulary the child learns
by collecting coins, so matching runs against five candidates rather than against
open transcription. **The riddle scales by age**: one property in the clue is a
four-year-old's hint, two properties is a nine-year-old's inference. That is
where the older child's beat lives.

The likeliest real failure is not technical. It is a parent blurting the answer,
which is a line on the setup card rather than a code change.

---

## Turns are grouped, never alternating

Each child casts three times in a row, then the next child takes over.

This started as a story preference and turned out to be a technical requirement
too. Gesture templates are collected from the casts themselves, and
**consecutive strokes make better templates**: alternating turns every sample
into a cold start.

The obstacle is then tuned so it needs all of them. *"That's not enough. It needs
both of you."* That line is generated rather than recorded, because it depends on
how many children are playing.

---

## Two content channels, and the seam between them

**Recorded video is identical for every family.** It carries story, character,
comedy and emotion, and it can never contain a name, a pet, a room, or an
assumption about how many children are playing.

**Comic panels are generated per family** and carry the names, the pet, the
actual rooms of the house, the search instructions and the reactions.

An earlier version of the adventure predated this split, so everything the
character said lived inside a video, which meant every clip mentioned my own
children and my own rooms. **It was unshippable to anyone else and looked
finished**, which is the worst combination of properties a deliverable can have.

The seam also decides production cost. Six character videos of 30 to 60 seconds
each, and everything smaller is a panel. A pile of ten-second reaction clips is
the expensive way to buy less.

Panels are written once with tokens and rendered per family at the moment they
appear:

> "Is {pet} there? Don't let {pet} touch anything. I mean it."

A family with no pet does not get a neutral rewrite of that line. **They do not
get the line at all**, because the joke depends on the pet having a name, and a
sentence with a hole in it is worse than a sentence that was never there.

---

## Confrontations are unlosable and untimed

The tension lives in the sound and in the character, never in a clock. A child
cannot fail the finale; they can only take longer over it.

This sounds like it removes stakes. In practice the stakes for a five-year-old
are entirely supplied by the story, and a countdown mostly supplies anxiety plus
a chance to end the evening badly.

---

## The rule underneath all of them

**No beat exists for technical reasons.**

Every time a technical need tried to become a scene, it got absorbed into
something the story already wanted. Template collection became the character
teaching a spell. A microphone check became nothing at all, on the grounds that
if the microphone were broken, the very first thing the product would do is ask a
child to shout and then ignore them.
